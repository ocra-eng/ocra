Technical Officials — TOs — observe competition and apply the rules. They are the
reason an athlete can trust that the person who beat them completed the same
obstacles to the same standard.

:::not-live
OCRA is building the national pathway for Technical Officials. The levels and
certification requirements are still being finalised. This page describes the role
and how to start now; the formal structure will be published when it is settled.
:::

## What the job actually is

Technical officiating involves considerably more than standing beside an obstacle
and deciding whether somebody touched a bell.

Depending on the role and the event, a TO judges obstacle completion and records
failures and penalties, monitors lanes and course boundaries, supports the start
and finish, checks equipment and course areas, records incidents, supports timing
and results verification, answers athlete questions, and assists with protests.

The skill is in positioning, observation, consistency and communication — knowing
where to stand, knowing what you can actually see from there, and being able to
explain a decision to an athlete who has just lost a place because of it.

## Fairness is the whole job

Officials apply the same rules to every athlete, regardless of who they are, what
club they represent, how well known they are, or what position they are in.

Declare conflicts of interest before the competition rather than after. If you
coach someone in the field, say so.

Competition only works if athletes believe the standard was the same for
everyone. That belief is quick to lose and slow to rebuild.

## Safety

Technical Officials are not event safety personnel, medical staff or event
management, and should not act as though they are.

But officials are often the closest person to an athlete in difficulty. So it is
part of the role to spot an immediate problem, follow the event's safety
procedure, and know when competition continues, when something gets escalated, and
who has the authority to stop a race.

## Who it suits

You do not need to be an elite OCR athlete. You need attention to detail,
fairness, the confidence to make a call and stand behind it, clear communication,
impartiality, and a willingness to learn the rules properly.

The role suits current and former athletes, coaches, club members, volunteers,
race organisers, officials from other sports, and anyone drawn to the technical
side of competition.

## How you start

Entry-level education covers what you need to support competition safely and
consistently: the role itself, the basic competition rules, obstacle completion,
penalties, athlete safety, observation and positioning, recording decisions, and
professional conduct.

After that it is a practical role and practical experience is how you develop. New
officials work alongside experienced TOs at suitable events, building up across
different obstacles, race formats, athlete categories and competition levels.

Obstacle sport has several disciplines — see [rules](/compete/rules) — and
officiating them can require discipline-specific education. Whatever you
officiate, you are expected to know the current version of those rules, not last
season's.

## Where it leads

With experience, officials take on more: leading an obstacle team, supervising
sections of a course, managing start or finish operations, supporting results
verification, reviewing incidents, handling protests, briefing and managing
officiating teams, supporting National Championship delivery, and working at
international competition.

Experienced officials also mentor newer ones, which is how a standard actually
gets passed on. Rules and formats change, so development does not stop at
certification.

## Why do it

It is a way to contribute that does not depend on your own race results. You learn
obstacle racing in far more technical detail than competing teaches you, you work
alongside athletes and organisers, and the pathway runs as far as National
Championships, Team Ireland events and international competition.
