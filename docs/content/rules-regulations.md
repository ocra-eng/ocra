Competition rules exist so athletes know what is expected of them and Technical
Officials can apply one standard across a whole field. Different disciplines need
different rules, so what applies depends on the competition you have entered.

## The disciplines

OCRA publishes rules for the competitions it organises and sanctions. The
recognised formats are:

100m
: Short-format obstacle sprint. Compact course, high obstacle density, small
mistakes cost places.

400m
: Short-format racing over a longer sprint distance, combining speed with
sustained obstacle work.

Short course
: Running and multiple obstacles over a shorter endurance distance.

Standard course
: A longer endurance race requiring athletes to keep completing obstacles as
fatigue builds.

Team and relay
: Competed in teams or relay legs, with the structure set by the competition.

## What the rules cover

For any given competition, the rulebook sets out the race format and starting
procedure, what counts as completing an obstacle, whether retries are allowed,
the penalties for failing one, course boundaries, timing, categories and Age
Groups, athlete conduct, the authority of Technical Officials, how a result is
classified, and how to protest a decision.

## Completing an obstacle

This is the part that decides races, and it varies by discipline. Depending on the
competition, you may be required to complete the obstacle unaided, follow a
defined technique, stay within a lane, repeat a section, or take a penalty when
you fail.

:::before-you-rely-on-this
Read the rulebook for your competition before you race. Assuming the rules are
the same as the last event you did is the most common way athletes lose places
they had earned.
:::

## Technical Officials

Officials observe competition and apply the rules impartially. Athletes are
expected to follow the instructions of an authorised official during competition.

How officials are trained and what they are accountable for is on
[Technical Officials](/education/technical-officials).

## Protests and appeals

Competitive events give athletes a formal way to challenge a decision or a
result. Where a protest procedure applies, you will be told what can be
challenged, who may submit one, how and within what time limit, what evidence is
needed, who reviews it, and whether a further appeal exists.

Championship-specific procedures can override the general ones, and the
championship information will say so.

## International competition

International championships run under the rules of the relevant international
federation or the championship organiser. OCRA provides access to those rules for
Irish athletes where they are available, but competing internationally means
taking responsibility for knowing them.

## Current and archived rulebooks

Only the current rulebook for a discipline is in force. Each shows its discipline,
version and effective date.

Superseded rulebooks stay available for reference and are marked as no longer
current — which matters most where rules changed between seasons and an athlete is
looking back at an old result.
